There is a number I keep in my head: p = 5e-6. A confident result, written up cleanly, ready to inform a decision. The effect looked real, the math was tight, the chart was persuasive. Then a stakeholder pushed back — not on the conclusion, on a detail — and that was enough to make me open the raw counts and start over from the bottom.

I want to be honest about the ordering, because it is the whole point. I did not catch it in a flash of rigor. Someone asked an awkward question, and I had built just enough habit to take it seriously instead of defending the slide.

Underneath was not one mistake but three, stacked, each individually plausible. The chart I was reasoning from did not match the dataset I thought I was querying. A cumulative sum had quietly inflated the denominator about sixfold — every row counting its predecessors. And the metric was continuous with unequal variance between arms, the kind that wants a Welch-type correction rather than the pooled test I'd reached for. Peel all three off and p = 5e-6 collapsed to p = 0.59. Not a small effect — no effect we could tell from noise. The confident write-up was describing weather that wasn't there.

Wrongness, made visible early enough

The reflex after a story like this is to promise to be more careful. That is the wrong lesson, because careful people produced all three errors. The denominator bug looked like a correct aggregation. The chart mismatch looked like the chart. Carefulness is no defense against mistakes that are shaped like correctness.

So I stopped thinking of experimentation as a discipline of being right, and started treating it as a system for surfacing wrongness. Good experimentation is not the absence of mistakes; it is making them visible early enough that nobody ships them. Trust, in this frame, is not a feeling you extend to a number because it has a small p-value and a clean deck. Trust is the output of an error-surfacing system — the audit trail, the second query path, the stakeholder allowed to ask the awkward thing. If the system has no way to make you wrong, its confidence is decorative.

And the traps run deeper than a runaway sum. The result that genuinely unsettled me ran against intuition: pull the users who flip between buckets out of an A/A test — the obvious cleaning step — and the variance goes up, not down. Heavy users, the ones most likely to be caught flipping, are exactly the ones whose behavior is stable enough to anchor a bucket. Strip them and you throw out the ballast. If a paradox like that hides where, by construction, there is nothing to find, imagine what hides in the live experiments.

The one orientation I'd keep if I had to drop the rest: prefer falsification to confirmation. The instinct is to hunt for the query that confirms the effect; the discipline is to spend your energy on the query that would kill it, and ship only what survives. A result you have honestly tried to break and could not is worth more than one you merely decorated — and when something does make you wrong, the right response is gratitude, not defense.
