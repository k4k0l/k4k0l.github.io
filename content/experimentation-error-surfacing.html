There is a number I keep on a sticky note, more or less, in my head: p = 5e-6. It was the headline statistic of a confident result, written up cleanly, ready to inform a decision. The effect was real, the math was tight, the chart was persuasive. A stakeholder pushed back — not on the conclusion, on a detail — and that push was enough to make me open the raw counts and start over from the bottom.

I want to be honest about that ordering, because it is the whole point. I did not catch it myself in a flash of rigor. Someone asked an awkward question, and I had built just enough habit to take the question seriously instead of defending the slide.

What I found underneath was not one mistake but three, stacked, each individually plausible. The chart I had been reasoning from did not correspond to the dataset I thought I was querying. A cumulative sum had quietly inflated the denominator by roughly six times — every row counting its predecessors. And the metric was continuous with unequal variance between arms, the kind of thing that wants a Welch-type correction rather than the textbook pooled test I had reached for. Peel all three off and the p = 5e-6 certainty collapsed into p = 0.59. A null. Not a small effect — no effect we could distinguish from noise. The confident write-up was describing weather that wasn't there.

Wrongness, made visible early enough

The reflex after a story like this is to promise to be more careful. That is the wrong lesson, because careful people produced all three errors. Each was the output of a reasonable person doing a reasonable thing. The denominator bug looked like a correct aggregation. The chart mismatch looked like the chart. Carefulness is not a defense against errors that are shaped like correctness.

So I stopped thinking about experimentation as a discipline of being right and started thinking about it as a system for surfacing wrongness. Good experimentation is not the absence of mistakes; it is making mistakes visible early enough that nobody ships them. Trust, in this frame, is not a feeling you extend to a number because it has a small p-value and a clean deck. Trust is the output of an error-surfacing system — the audit trail, the second query path, the stakeholder allowed to ask the awkward thing. If the system has no way to make you wrong, its confidence is decorative.

The traps are subtle, and they compound

It would be comforting if the dangers were all as crude as a runaway cumulative sum. They are not. The deeper traps live in the assumptions you never write down.

The most common one is treating sessions as independent when your unit of action is a user. Run a clustered bootstrap-by-user over the data — in one case around 1.5M sessions across roughly 163k users — and the design effect comes out near 12.5, with an intraclass correlation around 0.40. Translated: your true sample size is a fraction of the row count you've been quoting, and confidence intervals computed on raw sessions are far too narrow. Half your dramatic findings are heavy users repeating themselves, and the naive test cannot tell the difference.

Then there is the result that genuinely unsettled me, because it ran against intuition. Users who flip between buckets across the experiment look like contamination — surely removing them cleans the data. But pull the flip-floppers out of an A/A test and the variance goes up. The reason is the law of large numbers: heavy users, the ones most likely to be observed flipping, are exactly the ones whose behavior is stable enough to anchor a bucket. Their flips add noise, not bias. Strip them and you discard the ballast. The "obvious" data-cleaning step makes your null test less trustworthy, not more. If a paradox like that can hide in an A/A test — where, by construction, there is nothing to find — imagine what hides in the live ones.

A stance you can hand to someone else

The output of that audit was not a resolution to try harder. It was infrastructure. A verification checklist that runs before any result becomes a claim — reconcile the chart to the dataset, recompute one metric from raw counts by hand, check the independence assumption before quoting any interval, ask which test the variance structure actually requires. And a banned-overclaim lexicon: a short list of phrases — "clearly," "proves," "X% lift" stated without a denominator — that flag a sentence as having outrun its evidence. None of it is clever. That is the feature. Cleverness does not survive a tired Friday afternoon; a checklist does.

Underneath all of it is a single orientation, the one I'd keep if I had to throw the rest away: prefer falsification to confirmation. The instinct is to go looking for the query that confirms the effect. The discipline is to spend your energy looking for the query that would kill it, and to ship only what survives the attempt. A result you have honestly tried to break and could not is worth more than a result you merely decorated.

Campbell's Law warns that any metric used for decision-making will eventually be gamed, including by the well-meaning analyst gaming it against themselves without noticing. The countermeasure is not virtue. It is building the thing that makes the gaming visible — and then being grateful, not defensive, when it does.
