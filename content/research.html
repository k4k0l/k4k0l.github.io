Research roots

I started in academia, not in production. For roughly five years at PJAIT I worked on web-content credibility and crowdsourced quality assessment — social informatics and web mining, the unglamorous question of whether you can get a crowd of non-experts to reliably tell you which pages on the web are trustworthy, and how to measure when they can't. About nine papers between 2013 and 2018, one of them a credibility corpus that other people went on to cite more than anything else I wrote. That corpus is the part of my old work that still earns its keep, which feels about right: the most useful thing a researcher can leave behind is usually a clean dataset and an honest description of how it was made.

I want to be precise about the framing, because it is easy to oversell. These are roots, not an active academic program. I do not publish anymore, I do not review, I am not chasing a line of citations. But the questions did not leave me when I left the university — they just changed clothes. The thirteen-year thread runs straight: content credibility and quality evaluation, then recommendation quality and the discipline of A/B testing, and now LLM-as-arbiter content systems, where a model is asked to make the same kind of judgement a crowd worker once made — is this credible, does this belong, how confident should we be — except faster, cheaper, and with new and more interesting ways of being confidently wrong.

What carried over was never a particular technique. It was a habit of mind: treat every quality signal as suspect until you have looked at how it was produced, and assume the measurement is the hard part, not the model. The arbiter prompts I work on today are the same problem I had with crowd labels a decade ago — the answer is only as good as your ability to surface when it is bad.

A fuller list of the old papers lives on my Google Scholar profile, for anyone who wants the primary sources rather than my retrospective gloss on them.

Reading log

A few books that actually changed how I work, with short, opinionated takes. No "recommended reading list" theatre — just the ones I keep reaching for.

Slow Productivity — Cal Newport

The permission slip I needed to stop measuring myself in tickets closed per week. "Do fewer things, work at a natural pace, obsess over quality" reads as obvious until you try to actually run a team that way against a backlog.

Slack — Tom DeMarco

The argument that a fully utilized organization is a brittle one. I think about this every time someone proposes to fill the last 5% of everyone's calendar — that slack is the capacity to respond, not waste to be optimized away.

The Innovator's Dilemma — Clayton Christensen

Why competent, well-run companies lose to worse products: because doing the rational thing for your current customers is exactly what blinds you. Useful as a check on my own instinct to keep polishing what already works.

Originals — Adam Grant

A corrective to the lone-genius myth — originality is mostly volume, timing, and the willingness to voice the second idea after the obvious first one. The part on dissent as an organizational asset has aged well.

A Garbage Can Model of Organizational Choice — Cohen, March & Olsen

The paper that made organizational politics legible to me. Decisions are not the output of orderly problem-solving; they are collisions of problems, solutions, and people looking for an occasion to act. Once you see it, you cannot unsee it in any meeting.

Psychological safety — Amy Edmondson

The research backbone under everything I believe about error-surfacing. Teams do not get safer by being told to trust each other; they get safer when admitting a mistake stops being punished. Trust is a system, not a feeling.

The Master Algorithm — Pedro Domingos

A map of the machine-learning tribes that helped me place my own work and its blind spots. Dated in the particulars now, but the taxonomy still clarifies arguments that otherwise go in circles.

Campbell's Law — Donald Campbell

"The more any quantitative indicator is used for decision-making, the more it will be corrupted." I keep this taped to the inside of my skull whenever a metric becomes a target. It is the whole reason experimentation integrity is a discipline and not a formality.
