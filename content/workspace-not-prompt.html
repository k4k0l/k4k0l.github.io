There is a whole genre of advice about prompting now — frameworks, role-play incantations, the right way to ask a model to take a deep breath and think step by step. I have read a fair amount of it. Most of it is true and almost none of it matters, because the prompt is the part of the system with the shortest half-life. You write it, you get an answer, it evaporates. Nothing accumulates.

The thing that accumulates is the workspace. That is the unit I have come to care about: the durable, agent-readable context an organization carries around with it. Not the question you ask today, but the substrate that makes the answer worth trusting tomorrow.

What the workspace actually is

For the last while I have been building one — an internal, AI-augmented knowledge system, an exocortex of sorts, for a stack that has long since stopped fitting in any single person's head. The premise is unglamorous. An organization's real knowledge is scattered across wikis nobody updates, threads nobody can find, and a handful of experts who are tired of answering the same question. Most of it is stale, locked in a head, or both. The bet is that you can compress that into context an agent can read, and that doing so lowers the cognitive load enough for people to work more independently in a complicated system.

Mechanically it is not exotic. It is analyses written down instead of remembered. Handoffs between sessions and between people. Operational recipes — the actual sequence of commands that fixes the thing at 2am, not the idealized one from the runbook. Mirrors of the org's scattered sources, pulled into one place that an agent can search. Reusable agent skills, so a procedure done once becomes a procedure done reliably. And a verification gate: nothing becomes shared canon until it has been checked, because an exocortex that confidently remembers wrong things is worse than no exocortex at all.

None of those pieces is a prompt. The prompt is just the last six inches of the pipe. Everything load-bearing happens upstream, in the context the model gets to stand on.

The division of labor that this implies

Once you frame it this way, the role of the AI shifts. The model is the executor. I am the architect and the reviewer. That is not modesty; it is where the leverage is. A capable model with a thin, ad-hoc context will produce fluent, plausible, occasionally catastrophic output. The same model standing on a well-maintained workspace will produce something you can actually route to a colleague without re-checking every line.

The interesting part is that this is the same pattern everywhere I have applied it. Drafting a long piece of fiction with adversarial critique rounds and a consistency-checking layer. A networked game whose bots run on behavior trees stitched to an LLM. A small robotics build with my kids, sensors talking to a model. A homework pipeline that turns photos into source-checked flashcards. The domains have nothing in common; the build is identical. Context first, executor second, reviewer last. When the same shape keeps showing up across unrelated problems, that repetition is the signal. It is telling you where the real abstraction lives.

Where I have to be honest

I will not pretend the workspace thesis is fully vindicated, because the data does not let me. The system I have been describing is, to a large degree, aspirational and single-operator. The bus factor is roughly one — me. I have no clean quantitative measure of adoption, which is a polite way of saying I cannot prove other people use it the way I imagine they do.

Worse, and more instructively: in at least one logged session, the retrieval layer I had carefully built went entirely unqueried. The context was present and the behavior did not change. That gap — between availability and use — is the whole game, and I had quietly assumed it away. Presence is not behavior change. Building the library does not mean anyone walks into it. If you take one uncomfortable thing from this note, take that one, because it is the failure mode nobody puts in the demo.

The practical implication

So here is the contrarian part, said plainly. If you are building with AI and you are spending your energy on prompt craft, you are optimizing the cheapest, most disposable layer in the stack. The durable returns are in the boring infrastructure: capturing what you learn in a form an agent can read, gating it so the wrong things don't become canon, and — this is the hard one — designing for whether the context actually gets used, not merely whether it exists.

The prompt is a sentence. The workspace is a memory. One of those is worth maintaining for years, and it is not the sentence.
