    Am I Still a Data Scientist?

    2026-06-06 · field note · #career #AI #engineering-management #data-science

    The question comes up more than I expected — from a recruiter, a friend, and most often from me, somewhere between the fourth status meeting and the evening when I finally get to build something. Am I still a data scientist? It depends on what "am" means, and that stopped being obvious years ago.

    By title, I manage a small DS team. Fifteen-odd years across research, web products, data science, and finally leading people — each felt like the destination at the time. None turned out to be one.

    Manager versus maker

    Management is sold as the natural order: you do the technical work well, so now you make sure others do. A fair trade only if you like what you get back. I liked some of it — growing people, shielding the team, turning organizational chaos into something workable. The rest — politics, endless alignment, garbage-can decisions where problem and solution merely happen to meet in the same room — wore me down. I'm a player-coach, which sounds tidy until you count: I generate roughly twice the work assigned to me and push most of it outward. That's the job. But the maker in me went hungry, and a year ago I caught myself wanting to go hands-on again — not nostalgia, just the sense that it's where I actually move things.

    What AI changed

    I won't say AI changed everything; that's a slogan, and I distrust slogans. Something narrower and truer: it gave me back the maker half — not by writing my code, but by moving the bottleneck from execution to architecture and review, the things I'm good at and like doing. The unit of work is no longer the prompt. It's the workspace: durable, agent-legible context — analyses, handoffs, operational recipes, a verification gate before anything becomes shared canon. AI executes; I architect and review. The same pattern recurs off the clock — a hard-SF novel finished through rounds of adversarial critique, a networked game with behavior-tree bots, an unfinished robot built with my kids (the bottleneck was soldering, not the model). The recurrence is itself the signal.

    What I actually do

    My strongest thread isn't about models. It's about making a mistake visible before someone ships it. I remember a result reported with confidence at p=5e-6 that, audited down to raw counts, came out null — p=0.59 — three errors stacked: a chart that didn't match the dataset, a cumulative sum inflating the denominator, a continuous metric begging for correction. The audit started with someone else's question, not my vigilance. Good. Solid experimentation isn't never being wrong; it's being wrong visibly, early. Trust isn't a feeling — it's a system for surfacing error.

    Thirteen years ago, in research, I worked on assessing the credibility of web content. Then recommendation quality and A/B tests. Now, systems where an LLM is the arbiter of content. From a distance it's one thread: how to judge what can be trusted, and how not to be fooled by your own numbers.

    So am I still a data scientist? The label stopped being enough. Closer to the truth: I help people move forward when systems — technical and organizational — grow too complex to hold in one head. I build things with memory. I recover agency where complexity eats it. The title is HR's business. This part is mine.
